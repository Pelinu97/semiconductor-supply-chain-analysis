# Executive Summary:
Turkey occupies a structurally unique position as a NATO member maintaining diversified economic and diplomatic relations with both the United States and China. The intensifying US–China semiconductor rivalry presents Turkey with systemic risks concentrated in defense and industrial supply chains, alongside narrow but real opportunities in assembly/testing/packaging (ATP) and defense-critical design capability. A key finding of this assessment is that recent assessments, including those by the European Court of Auditors, raise significant questions about whether the EU Chips Acts eill achieve its 2030 objectives. This may affect the long-term strategic value of EU-based semiconductor diversification for partner countries, including Turkey. 

# Turkey's Current Semiconductor Landscape:
No domestic advanced semiconductor fabrication capability
High dependency on imported chips across defense, automotive, and consumer sectors
Turkey's defense industry relies on imported semiconductor components for many advanced electronic systems.
The 2020–2022 global semiconductor shortage disrupted Turkish automative production, illustrating the  country's exposure to external supply-chain shocks. 

# Strategic Risks for Turkey:

Defense Industry Exposure:
Advanced Turkish defense systems, including UAV platforms, continue to rely on certain imported semiconductor components and electronic subsystems. Export restrictions or supply chain disruptions could affect production timelines, manintenance cycles, and supply-chain resilience.

Economic Vulnerability:
Turkey's industrial base is highly chip-dependent. A major disruption in East Asian semiconductor supply chains — whether caused by Taiwan Strait tensions, export-control escalation, or natural/infrastructure shocks — could create secondary effects on Turkish manufacturing and export sectors.

NATO Alignment Pressures:
Turkey's engagement with both Western and Chinese technology ecosystems creates a complex policy environment as a NATO-aligned semiconductor security frameworks become more restrictive. 

# Strategic Opportunities for Turkey
Given the high capital and technological barriers associated with leading-edge wafer fabrication, semiconductor assembly, testing, and packaging (ATP) represent a more accessible entry point for Turkey to participate in global semiconductor supply chains. Existing industrial capabilities in electronics manufacturing could provide a foundation for further development in this area.

# EU Chips Act Alignment — Revised Outlook:
The EU Chips Act aims to increase Europe's share of global semiconductor production to 20% by 2030. Recent assessments, including analysis from the European Court of Auditors, indicate significant challenges in achieving this objective. Public estimates suggest that the EU may fall below the original target without substantially increased investment and implementation capacity.

For Turkey, this does not eliminate potential opportunities for cooperation with European semiconductor initiatives, but it changes the strategic context. Rather than relying on a rapidly expanding European semiconductor ecosystem, Turkish engagement should be assessed within a longer-term and a more gradual and capacity-constrained European industrial policy environment.

# Domestic R&D Development:
Institutions including ASELSAN and TÜBİTAK have been involved in semiconductor-related research and technology development activities. Scaling these programs could reduce long-term dependency in defense-critical applications, though design capabilities alone would not eliminate the broader dependency created by the lack of domestic advanced fabrication capacity. 

# Geopolitical Positioning:
Turkey's position between multiple geopolitical and economic networks may provide limited diplomatic flexibility in technology-related engagements, potentially creating access advantages in certain procurement channels. However, this advantage should be viewed as limited and context-dependent and not a substitute for domestic capability development.

#Assessment:
Turkey's most realistic near-term opportunity is unlikely to be advanced fabrication entry, but rather defense-critical risk reduction and supply-chain resilience. Given that even the United States and EU — despite significantly larger capital bases — continue to face challenges in reducing dependence on Taiwan-concentrated advanced manufacturing, Turkey's comparative advantage lies in narrower and more achievable objectives: ATP-stage integration, development of design-level capabilities for defense-critical components where volumes are lower and strategic value is higher, and diversified sourcing strategies rather than domestic leading-edge fabrication.

The EU Chips Act's implementation challenges suggest that Turkey should treat European alignment as one diversification channel among several rather than a single primary strategy, while maintaining engagement through bilateral and NATO-related frameworks.

Confidence level: Moderate — structural risks are well documented in open sources; opportunity assessments involve greater analytical judgment and remain more contestable.

# Recommendations
Establish a national semiconductor strategy with defined long-term targets, calibrated to realistic capability tiers with near-term focus on ATP and design capabilities rather than a leading-edge fabrication. 

Strengthen domestic chip design capabilities through academia–industry collaboration, prioritizing defense-critical applications
Engage with EU semiconductor initiatives and working groups, while tracking the EU's 2026–2027 Chips Act review for revised terms of engagement

Diversify semiconductor supply chain dependencies across allied and non-aligned vendors
Develop strategic reserves for critical defense semiconductor components

# Conclusion:
Semiconductors have evolved from an economic commodity into a core national security asset. For Turkey, this structural shift necessitates proactive industrial policy, supply chain diversification, and targeted capability development to mitigate external dependencies while leveraging geopolitical positioning. Turkey's strategy should be calibrated to its actual capability tier — design and assembly, not fabrication — and should avoid relying exclusively on EU frameworks, particularly as implementation challenges and target-achievement concerns have emerged in European assessments. 

# Methodological Note:
This assessment draws exclusively from publicly available institutional reports, EU audit findings, and open-source industry reporting. No classified, proprietary, or non-public information was used. Opportunity-related judgments in this report reflect analytical reasoning rather than confirmed policy outcomes and should be read as indicative.
